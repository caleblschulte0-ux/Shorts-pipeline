# DATA EXPLAINER — STORY-FIRST SCRIPT PLAYBOOK
### Research-backed editorial operating system | Version 1.0 | November 14, 2026

**Purpose:** Replace mechanical “stat, stat, stat, takeaway” scripts with short stories that give viewers a reason to stop, stay, understand, and share. This is a creative standard and implementation specification, **not** a promise of viral reach.

**Applies to:** Data Explainer's illustrated/data shorts and its emerging 3D mascot shorts. The same editorial principles apply; production formats may differ.

**Core editorial command:** **Find the human story first. Use verified data to prove it. Make the proof visual. End when the promise has been fulfilled.**

---

## 1. What the research actually says

There is no public, universally reliable algorithmic threshold that guarantees a hit. YouTube describes recommendations as personalized and shaped by *whether a viewer chooses to watch, how long the viewer stays, and whether the experience is satisfying*. Topic interest, audience fit, and competing videos matter, too. Its Shorts Studio reports include **Shown in feed** and **How many chose to view** (watched versus swiped). These are different questions from average duration or average percentage viewed. Source: [YouTube recommendations](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16533387), [Search & discovery](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11914225), and [Shorts analytics](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217?co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts&hl=en).

**Avoid vanity analytics traps:** YouTube changed Shorts view counting in March 2025 so a start or replay can register as a view without a minimum watch time. The platform still distinguishes engaged views for important reporting. Do not judge a script from views alone. See [YouTube Studio Content Manager updates](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9082582).

### Findings from high-performing creators — and the lessons to borrow

## 2. The new north star

**A viewer must be able to answer three questions after the first two seconds:**
1. *What am I looking at?*
2. *Why is it unusual / relevant / worth knowing?*
3. *What am I waiting to find out?*

**A viewer must receive three things by the end:**
1. The promised reveal, explained truthfully.
2. A concrete mental image or model that makes the answer stick.
3. A sense that watching was worth the time.

Videos should be judged on **discovery and satisfaction**, not on whether they contain an arbitrary number of charts, words, or scene changes.

### Non-negotiables
- **No fabricated facts.** Do not publish illustrative/proxy figures as measured truth. Verify date, denominator, comparison group and source; include uncertainty when it changes the claim.
- **No bait-and-switch.** The hook creates an honest expectation; the story fulfills it.
- **No mechanical talking-point list.** Every beat advances, complicates or resolves the same question.
- **No empty spectacle.** Motion must demonstrate a fact or intensify a scene; a distracting camera move is not a story.
- **No unnecessary mandatory CTA.** Don't force a question or “follow for more” after a satisfying ending.
- **No rigid universal duration.** Find the shortest length that properly delivers the story; benchmark by format and length.

## 3. Choose topics by audience demand, not data availability

A dataset is an *ingredient*, not an idea. Before drafting, prepare a **one-page idea brief** with:

- **Topic:** What the research is about.
- **Audience belief:** What a normal viewer likely assumes today.
- **Surprise:** What the verified evidence says instead.
- **Stakes:** Why it matters to money, time, body, safety, relationships, wonder, or how the world works.
- **Visual proof:** The one scene that could make the idea obvious without narration.
- **Promise:** The specific question the video will answer.
- **Payoff:** The final realization the viewer walks away with.
- **Sources:** The primary source(s), dates, figures and limitations.

### Six proven-feeling angles to test (not guarantees)
1. **Personal consequence:** “Your state may owe you money.” Best when a viewer can take action.
2. **Expectation reversal:** “Your creative side isn't located in your right hemisphere.” Best when a familiar assumption is wrong.
3. **Absurd scale:** “A tiny spice jar costs this much work.” Best when a number can become a physical comparison.
4. **Transformation:** “A drone market went from toy to tool.” Best when something crossed a threshold or changed identity.
5. **Unsolved puzzle / prediction:** “What happens if we drop this?” Best when the viewer can guess, then see the answer.
6. **Consequence chain:** “One small measurement error can wreck a mission.” Best when cause, failure and outcome can be shown.

**Pitch gate:** Reject or re-research a topic if the only pitch is “Here are three statistics about X.” Not every interesting dataset deserves a standalone Short. Combining unrelated statistics never creates a narrative by itself.

### Test multiple premises before production
For each candidate topic, draft 5 distinctly different opening concepts (not merely 5 synonyms), such as personal, counterintuitive, visual-scale, mystery and consequence. Pick the one with the best truthful payoff and feasible visual—not the one with the biggest superlative.

## 4. Design the opening at the FRAME level

The hook is not only a sentence. It is **the first frame + first audible line + the promise they jointly establish**.

### The first-frame test
At 9:16 mobile size, muted, for one second:
- One instantly identifiable subject or event is unmistakable.
- There is an anomaly, question or consequence visible.
- Any text is short enough to read in one glance.
- The frame is about *this* story; it is not a generic establishing shot or a mascot waving.

### The spoken line test
Use 5–14 words as a **useful drafting range, not a hard validator**. Prefer natural speech. It must make the viewer curious about *what comes next*. A numeral is optional; a compelling premise is required.

**Hook constructions to rotate:**
- “You might already own something your state is hiding in plain sight.” [personal relevance; adjust factual framing]
- “This tiny jar takes a ridiculous amount of human labor.” [scale + visual]
- “These drones used to be toys. The numbers say otherwise.” [transformation]
- “Your brain isn't split into a creative half and a logical half.” [misconception]
- “A ship can be brand new and still have an old problem.” [paradox]
- “Which falls first? Make your guess.” [prediction, but only when the reveal justifies a question]

### Disallowed weak patterns
- “Today we're going to learn about…”
- “Did you know that…” as default filler.
- “The data reveals…” when the data itself is not the hook.
- Standalone percentage with no context or interesting implication.
- “This changes everything,” “Nobody is talking about…” unless specifically substantiated.
- A title/logo card before the actual scene.
- Withholding an answer the video never delivers.

**Promise fidelity:** A title, first visual, first spoken line and ending should be compatible statements about the **same single discovery**.

## 5. Build an arc rather than a row of facts

### A useful default for a 20–40 second explainer
These windows are **planning suggestions, not forced timing**:

| Beat | Approximate place | Narrative job | Example: saffron |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | opening 0–2s | Show the anomaly / make a promise | A tiny jar next to a huge heap of flowers |
| Stakes / orientation | ~2–7s | Make the unusual thing relevant and clear | “That jar comes from thousands of tiny threads.” [Verify scale] |
| First reveal | ~7–15s | Show cause/evidence; transform understanding | One flower exposes only three threads |
| Complication or escalation | ~15–25s | Introduce labor, difficulty, contradiction or cost | Flowers harvested and threads sorted by hand |
| Payoff | final ~5–10s | Resolve the promise with the best visual | Flower heap resolves into one tiny finished jar |
| Exit | immediately after payoff | Leave on satisfaction | No extra recap or routine engagement question |

**But/therefore test:** Read the outline. If every transition is an implied “and then here's another fact,” it is not an arc. Each new beat should naturally answer “but…”, “so…”, “which means…”, “except…”, “until…” or “that's why…”. Do not literally repeat these words every few seconds.

**Information-progress test:** Beside each beat write: *The audience believed ___ before this; they know ___ after it*. If the before/after is identical, delete or redesign the beat.

**Open-loop discipline:** One main curiosity question; at most one small secondary one for typical 30-second shorts. No deliberate confusion, no never-ending tease. You can partially answer early and reveal the more interesting consequence late.

### Eight story formats — choose what the topic actually supports
1. **Misconception → test → surprise → mechanism.** Best for science/body/physics.
2. **Small thing → massive hidden cost → physical scale.** Best for saffron, microchips, water use.
3. **Normal situation → invisible problem → real-life implication.** Best for unclaimed money or safety.
4. **Before → tipping point → after.** Best for history, social change, business trends.
5. **Ranking with a surprising winner.** Only if the ranking is itself enjoyable; show stakes, not just positions.
6. **Mystery → clues → revealed cause.** Best for strange natural phenomena.
7. **Attempt → obstacle → adjustment → outcome.** Best for demonstrations, experiments and mascot-led scenes.
8. **Comparison → contradiction → why both can be true.** Best for misleading averages, statistics and measurement choices.

Do not randomly rotate formats for variety. Pick by explanatory fit and audience demand.

## 6. Make numbers mean something

A number is important if it **changes what the viewer believes or feels**.

**Replace:** “Commercial drone registrations reached 453,635.”

**With:** “More registered drones are now used for work than for fun.” If verified, then show the exact numbers as the proof.

**Replace:** “Alcor has 222 cryopreserved patients.”

**With:** “Thousands can sign up to be frozen after death. Far fewer have actually been preserved.” [Only use exact counts once independently verified and appropriately dated.]

**Replace:** “The activity obesity index reached 297.”

**With:** An accurate explanation of what is actually being measured, who is affected, and why the gap matters. If the measure cannot be explained without a lengthy methodological detour, it may be the wrong statistic for a Short.

**Rule:** Each essential numerical claim needs (a) an easily understood denominator or comparator, (b) a visual equivalent, (c) an accurate source and (d) a reason it matters. Big numbers don't automatically equal stakes.

Prefer **one central number per narrative beat**, but do not prohibit more when a comparison genuinely requires two. Never create misleading comparisons just to get drama.

## 7. Write for eyes, ears and evidence together

Make the script a three-column storyboard:

| EARS — voiceover | EYES — exact visual action | EVIDENCE — source/claim |
|---|---|---|
| “One crocus holds just three saffron threads.” | Macro 3D crocus opens; Data physically removes one thread at a time, clearly counting 1, 2, 3. | Botanical source confirming 3 stigmas per flower. |
| “Each one has to be handled by hand.” | Heap of flowers processed; the work stays legible at phone size. | Source confirming labor-intensive production. |
| “That's why this tiny jar costs so much.” | Match-cut from labor/flowers to a small full jar on a balance. | Retail pricing and labor source. |

### Rules for the 3D mascot
- **Data is an actor, not a pointer.** He physically tries, discovers, struggles, compares, reacts and demonstrates.
- **One dominant visual action per sentence.** If the action can't be described clearly, the scene is underwritten.
- **Make the proof huge enough to read on a phone.** A three-thread concept fails if the threads are four pixels wide.
- **Show causes and consequences.** Threads must visibly leave flowers and enter a container; no magically appearing piles.
- **The strongest shot is built into the script before rendering.** Don't ask the rendering model to invent the payoff afterward.
- **No ornamental 3D.** More elaborate sets do not save a weak script.

### Rules for illustrated shorts
- Convert abstract quantities into objects, body movements, transformations, maps, scales, or familiar scenes when accurate.
- Charts can be excellent **proof**, but should rarely be the entire emotional structure.
- Use a repeatable visual language for brand recognition without repeating the same infographics every time.
- Each depicted comparison must be mathematically honest (scales, denominators, time periods, units).

## 8. Give the channel a human voice

Data Explainer should feel like a curious, smart friend showing you something surprising—not an AI reading an assignment.

**Target voice:** confident, conversational, specific, occasionally witty, easy to understand on a single listen, respectful of the viewer's intelligence.

**Change:** “The data indicates significant regional variance.” → “The average looks fine—until you see which cities get left behind.”

**Change:** “This statistic highlights the importance of proper measurement.” → “The answer flips depending on where you start measuring.”

**Change:** “What are your thoughts on this trend?” → Cut the CTA, or end with a genuinely unresolved choice the story earns.

Do not write every video with exaggerated stakes. Rotate emotional tone: **awe, delight, suspense, irony, discomfort, useful surprise, quiet wonder, humor**. Some concepts should be beautiful or satisfying rather than alarming.

**Read-aloud gate:** One human/voice test. If the narration sounds written rather than spoken, revise. Avoid stacking technical terms, unexplained nouns, or long-number sequences. Avoid telegraphing the next three facts.

## 9. Endings that earn a rewatch or share

Choose the ending that actually fits the video:
- **Reframe:** “Everest is tallest above sea level. Change the starting point, and the winner changes.”
- **Physical reveal:** Five seconds of frantic flower sorting resolves into a tiny jar of saffron.
- **Practical discovery:** “The official state unclaimed-property search is free.”
- **Unexpected consequence:** A measurement error becomes a visibly failed mission.
- **Wonder:** Reveal the scale of a phenomenon with a familiar object.
- **Funny callback:** Return to a visual planted at the opening, now reinterpreted.

**Cut immediately after the payoff.** Don't explain what viewers just saw, don't add obligatory thanks, and don't sabotage the ending with a generic engagement prompt. A well-earned question can work; it is not a required field of the creative brief.

**Share test:** Ask, “What exact sentence would a viewer say when sending this to a friend?” If the answer is merely “This is an interesting video,” the idea may not be sharp enough.

## 10. Rewrites from the current Data Explainer backlog

*Illustrative script treatments, not final fact-checked packages. Numbers are drawn from existing project drafts and require source/date verification before publication.*

### A. Unclaimed money
**Current hook:** “14.3% of Americans have money waiting in state vaults.”

**Stronger hook:** “Your state might be holding money that already belongs to you.”

**Arc:** Viewer relevance → examples of forgotten property → scale of unclaimed property → free official search → immediate ending.

**Visual:** Wallet forgotten in drawer → envelopes move into labeled state vault → search field appears. **Payoff:** A state search gives the viewer a possible action. Avoid implying a specific viewer definitely has money.

### B. Commercial drones
**Current hook:** “Commercial drones now number 453,635 and outnumber hobby models.”

**Stronger hook:** “Drones stopped being mostly toys. Now work is taking over the sky.” [Specify “registered drones” and verify which population/time period is compared.]

**Arc:** Toy drone introduction → work drone at farm/roof/site → registered commercial fleet surpasses recreational category → ordinary sky, changed purpose.

**Visual:** One drone changes costumes/attachments with a split-screen recreation vs work contrast. **Payoff:** The shift is from hobby to infrastructure, not a random registration statistic.

### C. Left/right brain myth
**Current hook:** “0 of 7 brain networks supported the personality myth.”

**Stronger hook:** “Your creative side isn't secretly hiding in your right brain.”

**Arc:** Familiar pop-psychology label → why it seems plausible → networks cooperating across both hemispheres → personality label falls apart.

**Visual:** Two halves with stereotyped labels fuse into an active connected network. **Payoff:** Specialized brain functions don't divide people into two personality types.

### D. Cruise outbreaks
**Current hook:** “Newer cruise ships still logged 20 outbreaks.”

**Stronger hook:** “A new cruise ship can't fix one very old problem.”

**Arc:** Shiny ship → contagious stomach illness spreading among many close contacts → outbreak context → hygiene/response is the critical issue, not just the ship's age. Distinguish reported outbreak counts from risk per passenger or voyage.

**Visual:** Clean ship vs invisible contact network growing. **Payoff:** Building newer ships doesn't make infectious disease disappear.

### E. Saffron 3D
**Current hook:** “Saffron costs more than any spice on Earth, and it's all picked by hand.”

**Stronger hook:** “This tiny pinch of saffron takes about 150 flowers.” [Confirm 150-flower-per-gram comparison for the exact pinch portrayed.]

**Arc:** Tiny jar → single flower, three threads → hours of individual picking → dawn harvesting window → flower pile shrinks to jar.

**Visual:** Put the **physical ratio** on screen. A close-up of Data pulling three clearly visible red threads is more persuasive than a generic field or an unreadable clock. End on the visual price/proof instead of a routine “Would you pay it?”

## 11. Story selection + editorial scorecard (100 points)

**Before production, score each premise and rough script:**

| Criterion | Points | What passing means |
|---|---:|---|
| **Premise and audience relevance** | 15 | One-sentence reason an ordinary viewer cares; not just a topic noun. |
| **First-frame + spoken hook** | 20 | Instantly legible, truthful, intriguing, matches promised payoff. |
| **Narrative progression** | 20 | Each beat changes understanding; no three-fact slideshow. |
| **Factual strength** | 15 | Source-backed, accurate units, denominators, dates, inference and uncertainty. |
| **Visual proof** | 15 | Visuals explain or demonstrate, not decorate; feasible on mobile. |
| **Payoff and closure** | 10 | The ending resolves the promise with satisfying surprise/meaning. |
| **Natural voice and personality** | 5 | A human could say it comfortably; no classroom conclusion or AI filler. |
| **TOTAL** | **100** | |

**Editorial gates:**
- **≥85:** Script is strong enough to storyboard and produce.
- **70–84:** Rewrite the specific weakness before spending render time.
- **<70:** Re-concept the story or choose another premise.
- **Any fatal condition:** hard stop, regardless of score: invented/misleading fact, false comparison, promised payoff missing, unrenderable core visual, materially confusing explanation, inflammatory deception or unsupported harmful claim.

These scoring bands are **internal proposed standards**, not YouTube's official thresholds. The scoring system should be adjusted after blind human calibration against actual channel performance.

### Blind editorial evaluation
Use at least two independent evaluations, and ideally a human sample. Show the premise, first-frame mockup and read-aloud script **without** the author's self-score. Ask: Would you stop? What are you expecting? Where did your interest fall? What do you remember? Avoid judges seeing the intended rubric before giving their first impression.

## 12. Analytics: distinguish getting a chance from earning attention

**Four separate stages:**

1. **Distribution opportunity:** *Shown in feed.* Low exposure is not proof the hook failed.
2. **Initial choice:** *How many chose to view* / viewed versus swiped. If this underperforms similar topics and formats, inspect first frame and premise.
3. **Continued attention:** Engaged views, average view duration, average percentage viewed, retention curve. If viewers start but leave at seconds 3–10, inspect setup, confusion, visual mismatch, or lack of progression.
4. **Satisfaction and advocacy:** Likes, substantive comments, shares, saves where available, subs gained, repeat audience. These signals are influenced by topic, audience size and intent and should not be reduced to a single magic formula.

**Important:** Since March 31, 2025 Shorts views can include starts/replays with no minimum watch time. Never confuse *view* with *engaged view* or interpret a 100%+ average percentage viewed as a 100% swipe-to-watch rate. Source: [YouTube updates](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9082582), [Shorts analytics](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217?co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts&hl=en).

### Current project baseline from Nov. 14, 2026 analytics snapshot
In `state/analytics_explainer/latest.json`, among **69** videos with **at least 100 views**, **at least 72 hours of age**, and reported average percentage viewed:
- Median percentage viewed: **61.4%**.
- **43/69** had average percentage viewed below **65%**.
- **10/69** reached **80%+** average percentage viewed.

These are descriptive, selected-sample baselines, not causal estimates or platform benchmarks. They exclude very small/new videos, and are not a representative claim about every Short. Compare future uploads against **matched cohorts** of length, format, age, and subject. Some fresh video analytics were incomplete in the snapshot.

### Example strong performers to reverse-engineer
- “NASA's Mars Helicopter Was Built for 5 Flights. It Flew 72.” — obvious setup and reversal.
- “9 Pitch Drops Took 84 Years to Fall.” — a single absurd, visual event.
- “The Rubik's Cube Record Dropped From 22 to 3 Seconds.” — familiar physical action and dramatic comparison.

**Do not conclude that a title alone caused performance.** Reverse-engineer the opening *video*, not just the headline, and compare distribution conditions.

### A 30-video test program
Produce **30 new videos** with pre-recorded script metadata and paired, honest creative hypotheses. Do not repeatedly repost identical videos as “A/B tests.” Keep format/duration/topic-mix comparable where feasible.

- **Batch 1 (10):** 5 familiar-stat hooks versus 5 concrete consequence/misconception hooks; both groups must be honestly written and fully developed.
- **Batch 2 (10):** 5 primarily verbal explanations versus 5 visual-proof-first stories, matched by topic type and available production assets.
- **Batch 3 (10):** 5 straightforward revelations versus 5 stronger-but-truthful reframe/callback endings. Keep duration similar.

Track: initial chose-to-view rate, mid-video retention, end retention, AVP, engaged views, shares per engaged view, comments per engaged view, views at fixed 24/72-hour and 7-day windows, and qualitative viewer comments. Set thresholds based on historical matching cohorts, not someone else's viral claims. Compare medians and distributions; log low-sample uncertainty. Don't call 5-versus-5 a statistically conclusive experiment.

**Weekly learning loop:** identify the **beat** that underperformed, not merely the whole video. Record a specific hypothesis (“intro repeated the title; viewers left before story began”), propose one creative change, test on the next plausible topic, then decide whether it generalizes.

## 13. Implementation roadmap in the existing GitHub pipeline

**Do not remove current technical validity checks.** Separate machine-schema correctness, source accuracy and creative quality.

1. **Create an editorial idea-brief stage before authoring.** For each candidate output topic, generate premise, viewer assumption, stakes, payoff, visual key, evidence, and 5 hook concepts. Reject weak pitches early.
2. **Change `scripts/author_stories.py`.** Remove the creative requirement that each story have *exactly four segments* and *two full sentences per segment*. Allow variable beats/length while keeping the runtime schema compatible with downstream rendering and while validating minimum proof and coherent arc. Do not make a new schema until consumers are migrated.
3. **Revise `shared/rewrite_mailbox.py` and `scripts/editorial_gate.py` together.** Preserve numerical-claim traceability, source verification and honest title/hook promises. Replace the **mandatory numeral in title or hook** with a *strong, concrete premise* rule. Replace title-to-every-topic lexical overlap with **semantic narrative continuity**. Keyword similarity is not a substitute for story coherence.
4. **Stop forcing generic CTA questions.** Make `question` optional at the creative level and suppress it when it detracts from the ending. Preserve schema-required empty fields if a renderer expects them.
5. **Add a pre-render human-story gate.** Require scorecard ≥85, a single-arc beat map, a concrete payoff, and an exact phone-size hero visual. This should not be reducible to simple keyword checks.
6. **Keep the evidence firewall.** LLM can frame facts, choose story and write words; it must not invent data values. “Illustrative” datasets are unpublishable as real facts unless independently replaced and verified.
7. **For 3D, enforce action-proof coordination.** A production scene's required hand/action/prop, focal framing, causal transition and payoff must be storyboarded in advance. Render and inspect phone-size proof before full production.
8. **Run a calibration phase.** Back-score 20 older scripts and at least 10 strong performers blindly, check where the rubric disagrees with viewers, revise the rubric, then activate the gate.
9. **A/B creative hypotheses and log honest results.** Store hook type, story structure, topic, duration, first visual, final beat, approval rationale and outcome.
10. **Don't silently deploy these changes.** Treat this as a specification for a branch/PR and regression tests. Existing automated publishing must not be broken by a prompt-only creative change.

## 14. Ready-to-paste editorial system prompt

> **ROLE: DATA EXPLAINER — SENIOR STORY EDITOR AND VIRAL SCRIPT DIRECTOR**
>
> You are responsible for compelling, truthful, visual-first short-form explanations. You are not a statistic summarizer. Your job is to identify a surprising, meaningful story in verified data and make people want to watch through its payoff.
>
> **Priority order:** (1) truth and comprehension; (2) audience curiosity and meaningful stakes; (3) visual proof; (4) narrative progression and satisfying payoff; (5) brevity, personality and shareability; (6) rendering/schema compliance. Never sacrifice an earlier priority to satisfy a later one.
>
> **Before writing any narration**, create a private working brief with: viewer belief; verified contradiction or discovery; why a non-expert would care; one-sentence honest promise; ending payoff; strongest phone-sized visual; source(s); claim limitations; visual production feasibility. If the story is only three unrelated facts, reject it.
>
> **Pitch at least five hooks** with substantially different angles. Do not choose a hook just because it contains a number. Score first-frame clarity, spoken clarity, strength of curiosity, stakes, truthful promise and alignment with ending. Choose the best viable option, not the most exaggerated.
>
> **Write the hook and ending before the middle.** Build the minimum number of beats needed to move from expectation to discovery. Every beat must do at least one of: complicate, demonstrate, overturn, escalate, clarify a necessary misconception, or deliver a partial answer. “And here's another statistic” is not progression. Use but/therefore causality when appropriate without repeating the words mechanically.
>
> **Make a synchronized three-column plan:** EARS (spoken words), EYES (specific visual action that proves or changes something), EVIDENCE (exact source and data supporting the claim). For 3D, the mascot must physically perform the idea rather than point at abstract text. For illustrated Shorts, turn quantities into explanatory movement, not decorative graphics.
>
> **Write naturally.** A smart curious friend should be able to say every line. Cut throat-clearing, filler, hollow hype, academic conclusions, generic question CTAs, excessive numbers, unexplained jargon and repetitions. Use suspense only if the video delivers the answer. Vary between wonder, surprise, humor, utility and dramatic consequence according to the story.
>
> **Protect facts.** Never fabricate numbers, quotes, studies, dates, rankings, sources, geographic scopes, baselines or causality. Do not present illustrative values as measured facts. If no trustworthy evidence supports the premise, rewrite the premise or refuse publication. Preserve technical schema requirements, but never allow a keyword or numeral heuristic to produce unnatural English.
>
> **Score the draft out of 100:** premise 15, first frame/hook 20, arc 20, evidence 15, visual proof 15, ending 10, voice 5. Rework under 85; reconcept below 70. Any invented/misleading fact, missing payoff or unfilmable core visual is a hard stop. Provide a short editorial reason for the score. After a pass, run an audio-only and muted-first-frame test. Keep the ending tight.
>
> **Output for approved scripts:** title variants; chosen premise; hook; beat-by-beat voiceover; timed visual storyboards; claim/source table; payoff; estimated runtime; risk flags; creative rationale; editorial score. If the implementation requires existing JSON fields, populate them without inventing schema. Keep editorial notes in a sidecar until the pipeline supports them.
>
> **Primary objective:** A viewer should finish thinking, “I didn't know that—and the way they showed it made me get it.”

## 15. Daily operating checklist

- [ ] Does the topic have an unexpected, truthful premise beyond a dataset?
- [ ] Is the first frame itself recognizable and interesting at phone size?
- [ ] Is the first spoken line both natural and honest?
- [ ] Can a viewer articulate what they're waiting to learn?
- [ ] Is there one central narrative and not a list of statistics?
- [ ] Does each beat materially change viewer understanding?
- [ ] Are visuals proving claims rather than decorating them?
- [ ] Are all numbers/date/scope/source references verified?
- [ ] Does the 3D mascot perform the meaningful action if present?
- [ ] Do the strongest spoken line and strongest payoff image land together?
- [ ] Can the video end earlier without losing meaning?
- [ ] Would someone have a concrete reason to share this with a friend?
- [ ] Did at least one blind editorial review happen before full production?
- [ ] Was the learning hypothesis and post-publication metric plan logged?

**Final rule:** Never ask, “Is this a good AI-generated explainer?” Ask, **“Would this story deserve a viewer's attention if nobody knew how it was made?”**

## 16. Views, shares, comments, subscribers: different creative jobs

A viral view is not a loyal viewer. An impressive short can entertain someone once and never give them a reason to recognize the channel again. YouTube explicitly advises creators to distinguish **new, casual and regular viewers**, build consistent formats or series, and make audience conversations part of the channel. Sources: [YouTube's audience-building guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13615784?hl=en), [Understanding regular viewers](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10246996/understand-returning-amp-new-viewers-data), [Community comments for Shorts](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11913117?co=YOUTUBE._YTVideoType%3Dshorts&hl=en).

### Design for the specific action the viewer may take

| Desired result | Why viewers might do it | Editorial choice | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Watch** | They understand an unusual promise and want to see it resolved | Clear first image, compelling unanswered question, progressive evidence | Confusing premise, title/logo intro, facts with no stakes |
| **Rewatch** | A dense but comprehensible visual has a satisfying reveal, or a callback makes more sense on second viewing | Strong visual transformation, clean reveal, optional ending-to-opening continuity | Confusing viewers on purpose, microscopic captions |
| **Share** | They learn something a friend would be delighted, shocked or helped to learn | Useful discovery, credible surprise, funny/beautiful visual, social relevance | Begging “send this to 10 people,” manipulative fear |
| **Comment** | There is a sincere, specific choice, prediction or debate with more than one defensible view | Invite a prediction *before* reveal, or ask a concrete question in a pinned comment after the video | Generic “What do you think?” on every short, polarizing outrage bait |
| **Subscribe/return** | They know what sort of interesting discoveries they'll get next and recognize the channel | Consistent visual voice, recurring curiosity series, a mascot with authentic personality | Random subjects with no consistent editorial identity |
| **Save/use later** | The video supplies actionable information, a method, or a reference | Official lookup links, how-to steps, a memorable useful comparison | Needlessly withholding a useful answer |

**Engagement rule:** A CTA should amplify a real viewer motivation already present in the story—not introduce a new one in the final seconds. If the best ending is a visual reveal, end there. Use the pinned comment or description for a thoughtful follow-up prompt and source link.

### Possible recurring franchises

- **“You Think It's X”** — an intuitive belief is disproved by a physical demonstration (Veritasium-style prediction, not embarrassing people).
- **“What This Actually Costs”** — reveal the labor, energy, time or waste behind familiar things; well suited to 3D Data.
- **“The Number That Changed Everything”** — one consequence-rich historical or scientific threshold rather than a statistics roundup.
- **“This Shouldn't Work”** — counterintuitive engineering/nature mechanisms proven visually.
- **“The Hidden System”** — concrete, useful explanations about money, infrastructure, consumer life or everyday objects.

Do not launch all five as mandatory formats. Pilot two that match strongest existing viewers, keep a recognizable tone, and track which videos produce *returning viewers and subscriptions*, not only starts. A series is an editorial promise, not a prefix slapped onto unrelated content.

### Titles, captions and first frames

For each story pitch **three different honest title treatments**:

1. **Consequence:** “Why Self-Checkout Can Cost Stores More Than It Saves.”
2. **Paradox:** “The Faster Checkout That Created a New Problem.”
3. **Concrete contrast:** “One Small Jar Takes Hundreds of Flowers.”

A title must accurately describe the video's payoff. For Shorts feed discovery, the **first frame** often matters more than elaborate thumbnail artwork; on channel pages, search and subscriptions, title and thumbnail can still matter. YouTube's Shorts discussion makes this distinction explicitly. Source: [YouTube Shorts deep dive](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/).

**First-frame packaging worksheet:**

- The one image: `__________`
- The one thing visibly wrong/surprising: `__________`
- On-screen text (ideally one short phrase): `__________`
- First spoken line: `__________`
- What a viewer expects by the end: `__________`
- Exact payoff scene: `__________`

**Editorial reminder:** A reliable, inviting channel with a distinctive point of view can outperform empty hype over time. Do not interpret retention as permission to exaggerate, spread rumors, overstate causal claims or portray speculative science as established fact.

## 17. The outlier-study protocol used for every month of scripting

Study strong *and* weak performers from (a) your own channel, and (b) a curated sample of successful explanatory Shorts and science videos. The goal is to learn **mechanics**, not copy lines, designs or creative expression.

For each sampled video, record:

1. **The one-sentence promise:** What outcome does the audience expect?
2. **First-frame subject:** What can be identified silently?
3. **First five spoken seconds:** What curiosity/tension is created?
4. **Hook type:** Personal, misconception, scale, puzzle, transformation, human story, useful action.
5. **Transition map:** At which point did the story reveal, complicate or invert something?
6. **Evidence map:** Which factual claim is made, where is the source, and what actually proves it visually?
7. **Payoff:** What changes by the end? Is it worth the setup?
8. **Length and format:** Do not compare 15-second jokes with 50-second educational stories without accounting for those differences.
9. **Observed data:** Views at a comparable age, watched/swiped, engaged views, AVP, retention curve, comment quality and subscriptions *when available*. Treat public view counts as an incomplete measure.
10. **Transferable technique:** One specific, defensible lesson. Example: “Showing the result's scale before explaining the mechanism gave immediate orientation.”

**One-page teardown example — hypothetical:**

> Observation: The strong science Short begins with a massive physical comparison, asks the viewer to predict the result, and explains *after* the visible test. The weaker Short begins with source attribution and three setup facts, with no surprising visual until second 12. **Creative hypothesis:** Opening on the physical comparison instead of the context may improve chose-to-view and early retention on similar topics. **Next test:** Two future comparable stories, both accurately sourced, one with proof-first and one with context-first. Do not attribute the difference to a single change if the topics, distribution or visuals also differ.

Outlier study should produce **new ideas and controlled hypotheses**, never a pile of universal “viral” rules. The best creator method is a habit of close observation and deliberate revision.
